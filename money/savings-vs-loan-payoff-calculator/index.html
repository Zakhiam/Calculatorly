2Quick answer

The same monthly amount can either cut loan interest or earn savings interest, and the higher rate wins. Paying $500 a month to an 18% loan for 12 months saves $495.00 in interest, while saving it at 4% earns $111.23, so paying the loan wins by $383.77. Type your rates above to compare your two choices.

3The formula

Each extra loan payment cuts the balance, so it saves interest for the months left. Each deposit into savings earns interest for the months left. The loan balance is assumed to be large enough to take all the extra payments.

Comparing the two choices — in words: work out the loan interest saved by the extra payments, work out the savings interest earned by the deposits, and take the smaller away from the larger. loan interest saved = monthly amount × monthly loan rate × (months − 1) + (months − 2) + … + 0; savings interest = future value of the deposits − deposits paid in

Formula source: plain arithmetic on the four numbers you type, with monthly compounding for savings. The loan rate and the savings rate are the ones you type, and both are assumed to stay the same.

4Worked example: $500 a month, loan 18%, savings 4%, 12 months

  1. Loan interest saved: $500 × 1.5% a month × (11 + 10 + … + 0) = $495.00.
  2. Savings interest earned: deposits of $6,000 grow to $6,111.23, so the interest is $111.23.
  3. Difference: $495.00 − $111.23 = $383.77.
  4. Answer: Paying the loan wins by $383.77.

6Questions about savings vs loan payoff

Why does paying the loan usually win?

Because loan rates are usually higher than savings rates. An 18% loan charges far more interest than a 4% savings account earns, so cutting the loan saves more than saving earns.

When does saving win instead?

When the savings rate is higher than the loan rate, or when the loan rate is very low. A 3% loan against a 5% savings rate loses: saving wins by $56.93 on the same $500 a month over 12 months.

Does this cover the whole loan?

No. It compares the same monthly amount over the months you type. It assumes the loan balance is large enough to take every extra payment, and it does not work out when the loan ends.

Should I keep an emergency fund first?

Yes, in most cases. Paying a loan is usually better on the numbers, but money paid into a loan cannot be taken back in an emergency. Keep some savings you can reach before paying extra on a loan.

What about tax on the savings interest?

Tax can take part of the savings interest, which makes paying the loan win by even more. This page does not take tax off either side, so the saving side is shown at its best.

Is this calculator free, and do I need to sign up?

Yes, it is free, and no sign-up is needed. Nothing is uploaded: the calculation runs in your browser on your own device, and the numbers you type are not sent anywhere.
