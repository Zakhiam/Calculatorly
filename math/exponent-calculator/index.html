2Quick answer

A power is the base multiplied by itself as many times as the exponent says. For example, 2 raised to the power 10 is 1,024.0000. A decimal exponent gives a root: 9 raised to 0.5 is 3.0000, because 3 × 3 is 9. Type your own base and exponent above.

3The formula

Powers cover more than whole numbers: a negative exponent flips the power upside down, and a decimal exponent turns it into a root:

Power — in words: multiply the base by itself the number of times the exponent says; a negative exponent means one divided by that power, and a decimal exponent means a root. power = base^exponent; base^(1/2) = √base

Formula source: the standard definition of powers and roots. No outside data is used — only the two numbers you type.

4Worked example: 2 raised to the power 10

  1. Start with the figures: base 2, exponent 10.
  2. Multiply the base by itself 10 times: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2.
  3. Work it out in steps: 2² = 4, 2⁵ = 32, and 2¹⁰ = 32 × 32 = 1,024.
  4. Answer: 2 raised to the power 10 is 1,024.0000.

Try it in the tool above: type 2 and 10, and the result line shows 1,024.0000.

6Questions about exponent

What does a negative exponent mean?

It means one divided by the power. For example, 2 raised to −2 is 1 ÷ (2²) = 1 ÷ 4 = 0.2500. A negative exponent never makes the answer negative — it makes it a small fraction.

What does a decimal exponent mean?

It means a root. An exponent of 0.5 is a square root: 9 raised to 0.5 is 3.0000, because 3 × 3 = 9. An exponent of 0.3333 is close to a cube root. Type any decimal exponent and the calculator works it out.

What is anything raised to the power 0?

It is 1, for any base except zero. For example, 5 raised to 0 is 1.0000, and 100 raised to 0 is 1.0000. Zero raised to the power 0 is not defined, so the calculator shows a clear message for it.

Can the base be negative?

Yes, with a whole-number exponent: (−2)³ = −8.0000. With a decimal exponent, a negative base has no real answer — for example, (−4) raised to 0.5 would be the square root of −4 — so the calculator shows a clear message instead of a made-up number.

What if the answer is too large to show?

The calculator checks that the result is a real, finite number. A power that grows past what the page can show, such as 10 raised to 400, gives a clear message instead of a made-up number.

Is this calculator free, and do I need to sign up?

Yes, it is free, and no sign-up is needed. Nothing is uploaded: the calculation runs in your browser on your own device, and the numbers you type are not sent anywhere.
